Stanford Law School  ·  Office of the Dean

Statement of Dean Jenny Martinez on Freedom of Speech and Academic Discourse

In the matter of the disruption of the March 9, 2023 Federalist Society event

DATE March 22, 2023 FROM Jenny S. Martinez, Dean and Richard E. Lang Professor of Law TO Stanford Law School Community RE Freedom of Speech and Expression at Stanford Law School

I write to share my reflections and decisions following the disruption of a Federalist Society event at Stanford Law School on March 9, 2023, at which Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals Judge Kyle Duncan was the invited speaker. This event and its aftermath have been widely covered in the media and have generated intense discussion both within the law school and beyond. I want to explain what I believe Stanford Law School's obligations are with respect to freedom of speech and the norms of academic discourse, why I believe those obligations matter, and what steps I intend to take going forward.

First, I want to be clear: I am deeply committed to diversity, equity, and inclusion as core values of Stanford Law School. We seek to create a learning community in which people of all backgrounds can study and work together in an environment of mutual respect. I am also deeply committed to freedom of speech as a core value, and I believe that the two commitments are mutually reinforcing, not in opposition. A law school where only some views can be expressed is not preparing students to be effective advocates in a diverse democratic society, and it is not adhering to the standards of academic freedom that are necessary for a university to carry out its educational and research missions.

Fostering diversity, equity, and inclusion in legal education is critically important. I personally believe that our society benefits from having a legal system in which lawyers and judges represent the diversity of the people they serve, and that our laws and legal systems should work equitably for everyone. I also believe that diversity of viewpoint is crucial to a well-functioning democracy, university, and legal system. Equally important, I believe we must train lawyers who can engage with difficult and controversial issues, and effectively represent their clients and causes in a diverse, pluralistic society. The two commitments — diversity and free speech — are not fundamentally in conflict.

What occurred at the March 9 event, however, was not an expression of disagreement with Judge Duncan's views; it was a disruption that prevented him from speaking. Students who disagree with the views of a speaker have the right to protest, to hold counter-events, to write op-eds, and to express their disagreement in a multitude of ways. What they do not have the right to do is prevent a speaker from speaking at all. When protesters shout down a speaker or otherwise prevent an event from proceeding, this is sometimes called the "heckler's veto" — a concept well-established in First Amendment law and academic freedom doctrine. It represents the antithesis of free inquiry. A student who asserts the right to shout down speakers with whom they disagree is implicitly claiming that the same right should be available to people who disagree with causes the student may themselves care about — which would mean that no speaker could speak without the approval of the loudest opposition.

The protesters' actions at the March 9 event were inconsistent with our policies and community norms, and the disruption was wrong. I want to explain my reasoning in depth, because I believe the issues here are important not only for our law school but for society at large, and I think we can learn something from examining them carefully. The goal is not to punish students, but to help them become more effective advocates and citizens. Yet I am also aware that there must be accountability when our norms are violated, and I will address that below.

I. Why Free Speech Matters at a Law School

There is a reason why free speech and academic freedom are foundational commitments of universities across the political spectrum. Universities exist to advance knowledge through research, to teach, and to engage with society. All of these missions require a culture in which a wide range of ideas can be explored, tested, and debated. The right to speak, even on uncomfortable subjects, is foundational to this endeavor. This is true even when — especially when — the speech at issue is offensive or disturbing to some members of the community.

As a law school, we have additional reasons to take this commitment especially seriously. Lawyers and judges must be able to engage with ideas and arguments that they find abhorrent, because the zealous representation of clients and the fair adjudication of cases require it. A lawyer who cannot engage with arguments they find offensive will not be an effective advocate. A judge who cannot consider arguments contrary to their own views will not be a fair jurist. Legal reasoning requires the discipline to engage with views one finds deeply objectionable and to evaluate them on the merits.

Free speech protections exist in significant part to protect speech that some people find offensive or objectionable. This is a foundational principle of First Amendment jurisprudence. It is easy to support the free speech rights of people who say things we agree with; the test of commitment to free speech principles is whether we support those rights even for people whose views we find wrong or even morally repugnant. This does not mean that all speech is without consequences — speakers and listeners alike remain accountable to community norms of honesty and respect. But it does mean that the answer to disagreeable speech is generally more speech, not enforced silence.

The United States Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the government may not restrict speech simply because it is offensive or distasteful to some. Universities, and especially law schools, should model and teach these principles, not undermine them. When members of our community prevent speakers from speaking, they undermine the very foundation of academic freedom and send a message that the law school does not believe in the principles it is supposed to teach. Students who graduate from a law school that tolerated disruptions of that kind are going to be less equipped to argue in favor of those principles themselves when they are lawyers and judges.

II. The Heckler's Veto and Its Dangers

The concept of the "heckler's veto" refers to a situation where a person or group is able to effectively prevent speech from occurring by disrupting it, rather than by engaging with it. The term comes from First Amendment law, where courts have held that the government cannot silence a speaker simply because a hostile audience reacts to that speech. If the government could suppress speech whenever some members of the public objected loudly enough, then the right to free expression would be meaningless — subject to veto by whoever was most willing to make a disturbance.

The same logic applies in the university context. If students are permitted to shout down speakers they oppose, then the ability of any given speaker to complete a talk at a university depends not on any principled rule but on whether organized opponents are present and willing to make enough noise. This would mean that the most controversial speakers — often those with the most important things to say, whether one agrees with them or not — would be least able to be heard. It would create a dynamic in which those willing to use disruption to prevent speech gain a veto over the speech of others, which is antithetical to the free and open exchange of ideas.

I want to be clear about what I am and am not saying. I am not saying that students must silently acquiesce when speakers say things they find objectionable. Students have the right to walk out, to protest outside the event, to hold counter-events, to write responses, to engage in vigorous debate and discussion. All of these are forms of expression that are consistent with our values. What is not consistent with our values is action designed to prevent a speaker from speaking at all — to make it impossible for others who wish to hear the speaker to do so.

A student who believes that it is acceptable to shout down speakers they oppose must accept that others can claim the same right. Imagine that a student strongly committed to a cause saw speakers they supported shouted down and prevented from speaking by organized opponents. Most people would recognize this as a violation of the speaker's rights and a danger to the cause of free speech. The same logic applies when the shoe is on the other foot. Rules about freedom of expression need to be applied evenhandedly, or they are not rules at all but simply an after-the-fact rationalization for doing what one wished to do anyway.

III. The Administrator's Role at the March 9 Event

Several students have asked me why I did not attend the March 9 event, and others have asked me about the conduct of the Associate Dean who did attend. Let me address both questions. I did not attend because I typically do not attend student organization events, and there was nothing that indicated to me in advance that this would be different from other events. The decision about who attends such events as a matter of course is one that I have made based on my overall responsibilities, and I do not want anyone to read anything into my absence.

Regarding the conduct of the administrator who was present: I want to be direct. I disagree with the way the event was handled. A staff member or administrator who is present at a speaking event when disruption occurs should work to ensure that the speaker is able to speak and that the event can proceed in accordance with university policies. I recognize that this is a difficult and complex situation, and that administrators must make judgment calls in real time. But the message that was sent, whatever the intention, was not the right one for the circumstances. I have addressed this issue with the relevant personnel, and I believe it is being addressed internally and need not be addressed further in this communication.

What I can tell you is what I believe should happen when disruptions occur: administrators present should remind those who are disrupting that they are violating university policies, give them an opportunity to stop, and if they do not stop, work with security to remove those who are causing the disruption from the event. This is not a harsh response; it is a predictable, principled one that ensures that the event can go forward and that all students can make their own choices about whether to attend and what to think about what they hear. I understand that carrying out this kind of response in the moment is not always easy, and I appreciate the difficulty of that position.

IV. Academic Freedom and the University's Obligations

Academic freedom is a distinctive feature of universities that developed over time as a protection for scholars and researchers to pursue knowledge wherever it may lead, without fear of reprisal for their conclusions. It is the reason why universities are, in principle, different from other institutions — they are supposed to be places where even heterodox or controversial ideas can be examined and debated. The concept is closely related to, but distinct from, the First Amendment right to free speech. Academic freedom applies in the university context and reflects values specific to the educational mission.

Judges, like other public officials and figures, are legitimate targets of political and legal criticism and protest. Many students have strongly held and well-reasoned objections to some of Judge Duncan's judicial opinions and public statements, and they are entirely right to express those objections through legitimate means. The question here is not whether students have the right to criticize Judge Duncan — of course they do — but whether they have the right to prevent him from speaking at a university event to which others have also been invited. The answer to that question must be no, regardless of how strongly one objects to the speaker's views.

I want to say something directly to the students who were involved in the disruption, and to others who may feel that the principles I have set out here are in conflict with values they hold. I understand that many of you are deeply committed to social justice and have strong views about the harm done by certain judicial decisions. Many of you came to law school because you want to make the world more just and to advocate for people who are marginalized or disadvantaged. These are admirable goals, and I support you in pursuing them. But part of being an effective advocate is engaging with opposing views — understanding them, critiquing them, and making better arguments. The ability to do that is undermined, not advanced, by avoiding or shutting down opposing speech.

V. Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion

I have seen some commentary suggesting that the disruption at the March 9 event reflects a deeper tension between DEI commitments and free speech commitments at law schools. I want to address that directly, because I believe this framing is mistaken. Genuine DEI work is entirely consistent with, and in fact demands, a commitment to free speech and academic freedom. Diversity includes diversity of viewpoint; inclusion means creating an environment where a wide range of people and perspectives can participate; and equity means applying our rules consistently, not selectively based on the content of speech.

When an institution applies its rules about free expression in a viewpoint-discriminatory manner — permitting disruption of speakers with some views but not others — it is not practicing DEI. It is practicing a form of viewpoint discrimination that is antithetical to genuine inclusion and that will, in the long run, undermine the trust and credibility that institutions need to pursue DEI goals effectively. A law school that only allows some viewpoints to be heard is not a diverse institution, whatever else it may claim.

Moreover, the real-world contexts in which our students will work as lawyers and judges require them to function effectively in an environment of genuine diversity of viewpoint. Courtrooms, legislative chambers, negotiating tables, and other legal forums are not ideological monocultures. The lawyers and judges who are most effective in those environments are those who can engage respectfully and competently with people who hold very different views. Shielding students from that kind of engagement in law school does not prepare them for the world they will actually practice in; it puts them at a disadvantage.

I also want to note that there are many students, staff, and faculty at Stanford Law School who hold conservative or traditional views, who are members of faith communities, or who come from backgrounds where certain progressive assumptions are not shared. These members of our community have as much right to feel welcome, to participate in our shared life, and to express their views as anyone else. A commitment to inclusion that extends only to some and not others is not really a commitment to inclusion.

VI. Going Forward

Let me turn to what I intend to do going forward, both in terms of accountability for what happened on March 9 and in terms of institutional steps to ensure that we live up to our commitments.

With respect to the students who were involved in disrupting the event, the matter has been referred to the appropriate student affairs processes. I want to be clear about what I believe those processes should consider. Student conduct processes are intended to be educational as well as disciplinary, and I believe that is the right approach here. The goal is not to punish people for holding views I disagree with — that would itself be inconsistent with academic freedom — but to address conduct that violated our community norms and to help students understand why those norms are important. I believe in the ability of our students to learn and grow, and I believe that this is an opportunity for learning.

I also want to commit to some institutional steps. First, we will be distributing to all students our policies on free speech and the norms that govern speaker events and other campus activities, so that everyone understands what is and is not permitted. Second, I intend to create a series of educational opportunities — discussions, workshops, and other programs — that address freedom of speech and academic freedom in depth, including the history of these values and why they matter for lawyers and judges in particular. Third, I am going to be working with student organizations, faculty, and staff to develop better practices and protocols for speaker events, so that we are better prepared to handle situations like this one in the future.

I recognize that some people will find my approach here too focused on free speech and insufficiently attentive to the concerns of students who felt that their identities and dignity were at stake. I want to respond to that directly. I do not think that choosing free speech principles over a particular political orthodoxy, or applying free speech principles in a viewpoint-neutral way, reflects a failure to care about the concerns of students from marginalized groups. On the contrary, I believe that those students are better served by an institution that is genuinely committed to free expression, where their voices will also be protected, than by an institution that selectively applies free speech principles based on the political valence of the speech at issue.

History gives us ample reason to be cautious about arguments that the speech of some people can be suppressed for the good of others. The targets of such suppression have too often been precisely the people and communities who were already marginalized and who most needed the protection of free speech principles. The robust protection of freedom of speech and expression, applied evenhandedly and without regard to viewpoint, is the approach most consistent with genuine commitment to equality and inclusion.

Finally, I want to say something about the tone of some of the commentary about these events, both outside the law school and within it. Law school, and the practice of law, can involve high-stakes and emotionally charged issues where people's deeply held values and identities are in play. That is part of what makes law important. It is also part of what makes it hard. Learning to engage with those hard issues, to make arguments, and to listen to counterarguments, in a way that is both passionate and disciplined, is part of legal education. The goal of that education is not to produce people who don't care, or who don't have strong values; it is to produce people who can channel those values into effective action in a complex and contested world.

I am proud to be Dean of Stanford Law School, and I am proud of our students, faculty, and staff. I believe in this community's ability to learn from this difficult moment and to emerge from it with a deeper understanding of, and commitment to, the principles that make law schools, universities, and democratic societies work. I look forward to the conversations ahead.

Respectfully,

Jenny S. Martinez
Dean and Richard E. Lang Professor and Montgomery P. Stires Professor of Law
Stanford Law School
